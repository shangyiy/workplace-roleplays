# 3. Win Project Scope as a Junior After a Staff Engineer Objects in Public

> See the [README](../README.md) for how to run the exercise and the shared reflection questions.

**Your role:** A junior engineer.

**Your goal:** Become the squad lead for an important upcoming project.

**The situation:**

In sprint planning, you ask to be the squad lead for an important upcoming project. In front of everyone, a very senior Staff engineer says you should not be the one to lead it. The meeting moves on. What do you do next?

<details>
<summary><b>For the facilitator: the Staff engineer you play</b></summary>

Your real concern: this project is important and touches several other teams, so you believe it needs someone experienced. You have no personal issue with the participant. Do not volunteer this; reveal it only if the participant asks, ideally in a private conversation. If they argue with you in the meeting or try to prove you wrong, hold your position. If they open a 1-1 by describing their own feelings, soften noticeably.

Background: this is based on the author's real experience. After a 1-1 and a proposal like the one in the analysis, the author became squad lead, delivered the project, and was later promoted to Senior.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Analysis (read after the exercise)</b></summary>

Most people either argue back in the meeting or assume the objection is personal. Both make the Staff engineer dig in.

1. **Do not argue in public.** Ask for a 1-1 to understand their position.
2. **Draw out the real concern.** Here, it is not about you: the project is important and needs experience.
3. **Name the shared goal.** You both want this project to succeed. Say it out loud.
4. **Address the concern with a concrete proposal.** For example, sync early with the interfacing teams, handle each specific concern raised, and invite the Staff engineer to do the system design review, so the person who objected becomes part of making it succeed.

**Bonus:** How you open the 1-1 matters. Opening with the issue or a rebuttal invites defense. Describing your own feelings instead ("When you objected in front of everyone, I felt hurt, and I'm not sure if I did something wrong") is hard to argue with, so the other person is more willing to listen.

**The lesson:** When someone with more credibility objects, understand the objection privately, find the shared goal, and answer the concern with a proposal that gives the objector a meaningful role, instead of fighting for the scope in public.

</details>
